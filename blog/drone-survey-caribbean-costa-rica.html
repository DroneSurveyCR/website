Caribbean Costa Rica Drone Survey: Lidar Mapping for Coastal Development, Tourism Infrastructure and Port Logistics

April 17, 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  Drone Survey Costa Rica

Home › Blog › Drone Survey Caribbean

Costa Rica's Caribbean coast is a different world from the Pacific side. Dense tropical rainforest that stays green year-round. A coastline shaped by coral reefs, river estuaries, and mangrove systems rather than dry rocky headlands. A cultural identity rooted in Afro-Caribbean and indigenous Bribri heritage. And a development landscape that's accelerating fast — new tourism infrastructure, port expansion in Limón, and a growing wave of boutique hotel and residential projects from Cahuita south to Manzanillo.

For anyone building, buying land, or managing property on the Caribbean coast, the terrain presents challenges that the Pacific side simply doesn't have. Flat, low-lying coastal land with a high water table. Dense vegetation that makes traditional ground surveying slow and expensive. Rainfall that can arrive any month of the year. These conditions make lidar drone surveying not just useful — but often the only practical way to get accurate land data.

Tourism and Hospitality Development: The Caribbean's Biggest Growth Sector

The southern Caribbean — stretching from Cahuita through Puerto Viejo to Manzanillo — has become one of Costa Rica's most desirable tourism destinations. Unlike the more developed Pacific coast, this stretch has maintained a low-key, nature-forward character that attracts travelers looking for something different from the resort experience. That character is now driving a wave of boutique hotel, eco-lodge, and vacation rental development.

For developers and property buyers in this corridor, a drone survey is an essential first step. Here's why the Caribbean coast demands it more than most regions:

Flat Terrain and Flood Risk

Much of the Caribbean lowland sits at or near sea level. Elevation differences of less than a meter can determine whether a site floods during heavy rains or stays dry. Traditional property visits can't reveal this — the land looks uniformly flat to the naked eye. A lidar survey produces a digital elevation model (DEM) with centimeter accuracy, showing exactly which areas of your parcel sit higher and which collect water. This data is critical for deciding where to place buildings, septic systems, and access roads.

Dense Vegetation Hides the Ground

Caribbean properties are often covered in dense secondary growth, tall grasses, or mature tropical forest. You can walk a property and still have no idea what the actual ground surface looks like underneath. Photogrammetry — which uses cameras to build 3D models — only captures the top of the vegetation canopy. Lidar is different: laser pulses penetrate through gaps in the foliage and map the ground surface below. On the Caribbean coast, lidar isn't optional — it's the only technology that gives you a true picture of what the land actually looks like.

Protected Area Buffers and SETENA Requirements

The Caribbean coast borders several protected areas including Cahuita National Park, the Gandoca-Manzanillo National Wildlife Refuge, and Tortuguero National Park. Properties near these reserves are subject to buffer zone restrictions and must go through SETENA environmental impact assessments before any development. Accurate boundary mapping, topographic data, and drainage analysis from a drone survey provide the documentation SETENA requires — and help you understand your buildable footprint before investing in architectural plans.

Example project: A developer planning a 12-room eco-lodge on a 3-hectare parcel near Playa Cocles needed to determine buildable areas on a densely forested, flat coastal lot. Ground surveyors quoted three weeks and couldn't guarantee ground-level data under the canopy. We completed the lidar flight in one morning, and within four business days delivered a bare-earth elevation model showing a 40-centimeter elevation ridge running through the center of the property — the ideal building location, well above the seasonal high-water areas on either side. The developer used this data for their SETENA filing and architectural site plan.

Port Logistics and Industrial Surveying in Limón

Puerto Limón and the adjacent port facility at Moín are the heart of Costa Rica's Caribbean commercial infrastructure. APM Terminals' container port at Moín, which opened in 2019, handles a significant share of the country's import and export cargo. The surrounding area has seen increased industrial and logistics development — warehouses, container yards, access roads, and supporting infrastructure.

Drone surveys serve the port and logistics sector in several specific ways:

  • Stockpile volume calculations: Aggregate yards, coal storage, and container staging areas need periodic volume measurements for inventory management. Drone photogrammetry calculates stockpile volumes with high accuracy in a fraction of the time traditional methods require
  • Construction progress monitoring: New warehouse and logistics facility construction benefits from regular aerial surveys that document progress, verify earthwork quantities, and compare as-built conditions against design plans
  • Access road and drainage design: The flat, low-lying terrain around Moín requires careful drainage engineering. Lidar elevation data provides the precise grading information engineers need for road and stormwater design
  • Environmental compliance mapping: Industrial operations near the coast must document their impact on surrounding wetlands and waterways. Drone surveys provide current, georeferenced baseline mapping for environmental monitoring programs

If you're managing construction or infrastructure near the port zone, our construction drone survey guide covers the full range of applications for earthwork, progress tracking, and as-built verification.

Agriculture: Banana, Pineapple, and Cacao

Limón province is the center of Costa Rica's banana industry. Massive plantations operated by companies like Dole, Chiquita, and Del Monte stretch across the lowlands from Siquirres and Guácimo through Matina and Valle de la Estrella. Pineapple cultivation has also expanded significantly in the region, and smaller-scale cacao farming is experiencing a renaissance in the Talamanca corridor.

Drone surveys support Caribbean agriculture in ways that are particularly relevant to the region's conditions:

Drainage Infrastructure Planning

Banana plantations depend on extensive drainage canal systems to manage the Caribbean's high rainfall. Canal networks need to be precisely graded to move water efficiently without eroding. A lidar survey maps existing canal profiles and surrounding terrain with centimeter accuracy, giving engineers the data they need to design new drainage or rehabilitate aging systems. This is especially important after major rainfall events that can shift sediment and alter canal geometry.

Plantation Replanting and Expansion

When a banana block reaches the end of its productive cycle (typically 8–12 years), the entire area must be cleared, regraded, and replanted. A drone survey before replanting provides current elevation data for regrading plans and drainage redesign. For expansion into new areas, lidar penetrates existing vegetation to reveal the underlying terrain — slopes, depressions, and drainage patterns that determine whether the site is suitable for cultivation.

Cacao Farm Mapping in Talamanca

The Bribri and Cabécar indigenous territories in the Talamanca mountains are home to a growing specialty cacao industry. These small-scale farms operate on hilly, forested terrain that's difficult to survey by conventional means. Lidar drone surveys provide these farmers with accurate property maps, elevation data for erosion control planning, and canopy analysis that helps optimize shade management — all critical for producing high-quality cacao beans.

Navigating Caribbean Weather for Drone Surveys

The Caribbean coast does not follow the same dry season/rainy season pattern as the Pacific side. Rain can fall in any month, and the region receives significantly more annual precipitation — 3,000 to 4,000 millimeters per year compared to 1,500 to 2,500 on the Pacific slope. This is one of the most common questions we get from Caribbean clients.

Here's how we handle it: most days on the Caribbean coast have a flyable window, usually in the morning before convective showers develop in the afternoon. We study weather patterns for the specific week and location of your project, and we build flexibility into the schedule. For larger projects requiring multiple flight days, we plan consecutive days with weather contingency built in.

The Caribbean's driest months are typically September and October — counterintuitively, the wettest months on the Pacific side. If your project timeline is flexible, scheduling during this window gives the most consistent flying conditions. February and March also tend to offer good weather on the Caribbean coast, with the "veranillo" period providing stretches of dry weather.

DGAC Drone Regulations on the Caribbean Coast

Costa Rica's Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC) regulations apply uniformly across the country, including the Caribbean coast. All commercial drone survey operations require proper registration, operator certification, and flight plan approval. There are specific no-fly zones around Juan Santamaría Airport, but the Caribbean coast itself has relatively few restricted airspace areas, with the exception of the Limón International Airport approach zone.

We handle all DGAC compliance for every mission — flight plan filing, airspace coordination, and any special permits required for operations near restricted areas. For a detailed overview of the regulatory landscape, see our Costa Rica drone laws guide for 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a drone survey cost on Costa Rica's Caribbean coast?

Pricing starts at $1,000 USD for sites up to 5 hectares, with each additional hectare at $80 USD. Can you fly drones in the Caribbean's rainy weather?

Yes. The Caribbean coast doesn't have a well-defined dry season, but most days have flyable windows — usually mornings before afternoon showers develop. We monitor weather closely, schedule flights during clear periods, and build contingency days into project timelines. September and October tend to be the driest months on the Caribbean side, making them ideal for survey work.

What areas on the Caribbean coast do you cover?

The entire Caribbean coast — from Tortuguero in the north to Sixaola on the Panamanian border. This includes Puerto Limón, Moín, Siquirres, Guácimo, Matina, Cahuita, Puerto Viejo de Talamanca, Manzanillo, Bribri, and all surrounding areas. We also serve inland areas of Limón province including the farming regions around Guápiles and Pococí.

Can lidar see through the dense Caribbean vegetation?

Yes — this is lidar's greatest advantage on the Caribbean coast. The region's dense tropical vegetation makes traditional surveying very difficult, and cameras only see the canopy surface. Our DJI Zenmuse L1 lidar fires thousands of laser pulses per second that penetrate through gaps in the canopy to map the ground underneath. For Caribbean properties with heavy forest cover, lidar is the only practical way to get accurate ground-level elevation data.

Do I need a drone survey for a hotel or lodge development?

Highly recommended. The Caribbean coast's flat terrain, high water table, and proximity to protected areas (Cahuita National Park, Gandoca-Manzanillo Wildlife Refuge) mean that accurate elevation data and boundary mapping are essential for site planning. A lidar survey reveals drainage patterns, flood risk zones, and buildable areas — critical data for SETENA environmental impact assessments and municipal permitting.
