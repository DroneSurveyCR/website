"How accurate is it, really?" is the first question almost every landowner, engineer and developer asks before commissioning a drone survey in Costa Rica. It's the right question — a topographic map is only useful if you can trust the numbers when you design a road, size a drainage culvert, calculate earthwork volumes, or confirm a building sits inside its setbacks.

The short answer: a modern RTK-corrected drone survey delivers 2–5 cm vertical accuracy and 1–3 cm horizontal accuracy — accuracy that meets or beats traditional methods for the vast majority of projects. But that headline number depends on the sensor, the positioning system, and a small piece of the workflow most people have never heard of: ground control points. This guide explains exactly what drives accuracy, what GCPs do, and when you genuinely need them in Costa Rican terrain.

What "Accuracy" Actually Means in a Drone Survey

Accuracy is not a single number. Surveyors separate it into two distinct ideas, and confusing them is where most misunderstandings start.

Relative (local) accuracy

This is how correct measurements are relative to each other within the same dataset — the distance between two points, the height difference across a slope, the volume of a stockpile. Relative accuracy is what matters for cut-and-fill, drainage design, and contour generation. Drones are exceptionally good here because they capture millions of points in one consistent pass.

Absolute (global) accuracy

This is how correct each point is in real-world coordinates — its true latitude, longitude and elevation tied to an official datum (in Costa Rica, the CR05 / CRTM05 system). Absolute accuracy is what matters when you need the survey to line up with a cadastral plan, a neighbour's survey, or government GIS data. This is where positioning hardware and ground control come in.

The number that matters

For most Costa Rican projects, the figure to ask your provider for is RMSE (root-mean-square error) in vertical and horizontal, validated against independent checkpoints. A survey that reports "±3 cm vertical RMSE on 5 checkpoints" is telling you something real. A survey that just says "centimeter accurate" with no checkpoints is telling you nothing measurable.

What Drives Accuracy: RTK, PPK and the Sensor

Three things determine how accurate your final survey is, in roughly this order of importance.

1. Onboard positioning — RTK / PPK

A consumer drone with a basic GPS knows where it is to within a few meters. A survey drone uses RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) or PPK (Post-Processed Kinematic) positioning, which corrects the drone's GPS against a fixed base station to 1–2 cm precision. Our DJI Matrice 300 RTK pairs with a D-RTK 2 base station so that every photo and every laser pulse is geotagged at centimeter accuracy at the moment of capture. This single upgrade is what turns a hobby flight into a survey.

2. The sensor — LiDAR vs photogrammetry

The sensor decides what the drone can actually see. A photogrammetry camera builds terrain from overlapping photos, so it can only model the surface it can see — in vegetated land, that's the top of the canopy, not the ground. A LiDAR sensor like the Zenmuse L1 fires 240,000 laser pulses per second, and many of those pulses slip through gaps in the foliage to strike the actual earth beneath. In Costa Rica's forest, pasture and hillside terrain, that difference is the gap between a guess and a true ground model. We cover the trade-offs in depth in our LiDAR vs Photogrammetry guide.

3. Ground control and checkpoints

Ground control is the verification layer — the part that proves and anchors the accuracy. That's important enough to deserve its own section.

Ground Control Points: What They Are and When You Need Them

A ground control point (GCP) is a physical target placed on the ground — often a painted cross or a checkerboard panel — whose exact coordinates are measured with a survey-grade GPS receiver. During processing, the software matches those targets in the imagery to their known coordinates, locking the entire dataset to the real world and providing an independent accuracy check.

For two decades, accurate drone surveying meant scattering a dense grid of GCPs across the whole site before every flight. That's slow, labour-intensive, and in Costa Rica it's often dangerous or impossible — you can't walk a painted target into the middle of a closed-canopy jungle, a mangrove, or a near-vertical hillside.

How RTK changed the rules

Because an RTK/PPK drone already geotags every capture to centimeter precision in flight, the data is internally accurate without a ground control grid. That's why we can survey terrain where placing targets would be unsafe. What we still recommend is a small number of independent checkpoints — one to three measured points — used not to create the accuracy but to verify it and to tie the survey cleanly to the local CR05 datum. This is the "no-GCP" workflow that lets a single team map a forested finca in a day that a ground crew couldn't fully reach in two weeks. For the bigger picture on how this compares to traditional methods, see our drone survey vs ground survey comparison.

When you DO still want full ground control

A denser GCP network is worth it for the highest-stakes work: large infrastructure corridors, projects that must be merged with an existing official survey, legal boundary documentation, or any job where a third party will independently audit the elevations. In those cases the extra hour of fieldwork buys you a defensible, fully validated dataset. We'll tell you up front when your project is one of these.

Accuracy in Real Costa Rican Conditions

Lab numbers are easy; Costa Rica's terrain is not. Here's how accuracy holds up in the conditions we actually fly in.

Forest and jungle

This is LiDAR's home advantage. Where photogrammetry would map the treetops and miss the ground entirely, LiDAR penetrates the canopy and still delivers a 2–5 cm bare-earth model. Denser canopy means fewer ground returns, so we adjust flight altitude and speed to maximize ground hits — a planning detail that directly protects accuracy. We go deeper on this in our LiDAR canopy mapping guide.

Steep and mountainous terrain

Slope is where ground crews lose accuracy (and risk injury) and drones shine. The aircraft flies a terrain-following pattern that keeps a constant height above the surface, so point density and accuracy stay consistent from valley floor to ridge line — no interpolation across un-walked gaps.

Coastal and open land

On open ground, both LiDAR and high-resolution photogrammetry hit 3–5 cm comfortably. Here the deliverable choice is often driven by what else you need — an orthophoto for marketing, a dense visual surface, or a clean bare-earth model for engineering. Our topographic survey guide walks through which output fits which use.

How to Verify the Accuracy You Paid For

Accuracy you can't check isn't accuracy — it's marketing. Insist on these four things from any drone survey provider in Costa Rica:

  • A stated RMSE figure for both vertical and horizontal, not a vague "centimeter accurate" claim.
  • Independent checkpoints — points measured on the ground but deliberately left out of the processing, then compared to the model to prove the error.
  • The datum your data is delivered in (CR05 / CRTM05 for Costa Rica) so it lines up with cadastral and GIS data.
  • An accuracy report included with the deliverables, alongside the point cloud, DEM, orthophoto and CAD files.

Every survey we deliver includes this validation as standard. If accuracy matters for engineering or due-diligence on your project, that report is the document that protects you. You can read more about pricing and what's included in our 2026 drone survey cost guide.

One Thing Accuracy Can't Replace: The Legal Stamp

A high-accuracy drone survey gives you topographic data that meets or exceeds cadastral positional standards. But an official plano catastrado registered with the Registro Nacional must still be signed by a licensed Costa Rican topógrafo. The smart, fast path is to use the drone survey to capture the whole property at centimeter accuracy, then have the topógrafo verify and stamp the specific boundary pins — you get the data in a day and the legal signature on the part that requires it. Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is a drone survey in Costa Rica?

A modern RTK-corrected drone survey in Costa Rica typically achieves 2–5 cm vertical accuracy and 1–3 cm horizontal accuracy. LiDAR surveys with our DJI Matrice 300 RTK and Zenmuse L1 sit at the tight end of that range and can resolve the bare-earth surface beneath forest canopy, which photogrammetry cannot. These numbers meet or exceed the tolerances required for civil engineering design, drainage work, and cadastral support.

What is a ground control point (GCP) and do I need one?

A ground control point is a marked target on the ground whose exact coordinates are measured with a survey-grade GPS receiver. The points lock the drone data to real-world coordinates and verify accuracy. With onboard RTK positioning, many surveys no longer require a dense GCP network for relative accuracy, but one or two checkpoints are still best practice to validate the result and tie the survey to the official CR05 / CRTM05 datum used in Costa Rica.

Can you do an accurate drone survey without ground control points?

Yes. An RTK or PPK drone receives real-time GPS corrections in flight, so each photo or laser pulse is geotagged to centimeter precision without ground targets. For consistent 2–5 cm results we still place one to three checkpoints to confirm accuracy and anchor the data to the local datum, but a full grid of GCPs across the site is usually unnecessary. This is what lets us survey steep or forested Costa Rican terrain where placing targets would be slow or unsafe.

Is LiDAR more accurate than photogrammetry for surveying?

For bare-earth terrain accuracy in vegetated areas, yes. LiDAR fires laser pulses that pass through gaps in the canopy to measure the actual ground, so it produces a true terrain model where photogrammetry only sees the top of the vegetation. On open, hard surfaces the two are comparable, and high-resolution photogrammetry can even produce a denser visual surface. In Costa Rica's jungle and hill terrain, LiDAR is usually the more accurate choice for topography.

Does a drone survey meet Costa Rican cadastral accuracy requirements?

The topographic data from an RTK LiDAR drone survey meets or exceeds the positional accuracy used for cadastral and engineering work in Costa Rica. However, an official plano catastrado for the Registro Nacional must be signed by a licensed Costa Rican topógrafo. Drone data can supply the accurate base information and cut field time dramatically, but the legal stamp on the registered plan is still a human requirement.
