Who Regulates Drones in Costa Rica?

Drone operations in Costa Rica fall under the authority of the Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC), the country’s civil aviation authority. The DGAC operates under the Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MOPT) and is responsible for all airspace management, including unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) — the technical term for drones.

The DGAC’s regulatory framework for drones has evolved considerably over the past several years. Costa Rica adopted rules broadly aligned with ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization) standards, requiring operator certification, aircraft registration, liability insurance, and flight plan coordination for commercial operations. The framework distinguishes between recreational hobby use and commercial operations, with significantly stricter requirements for the latter.

In addition to DGAC oversight, certain operating environments in Costa Rica involve other authorities. Operations within or adjacent to national parks and protected areas managed by SINAC (Sistema Nacional de Áreas de Conservación) require a separate environmental permit from MINAE. Operations near critical infrastructure may require coordination with ICE (electricity) or other entities. Professional survey operators navigate all of these simultaneously.

Commercial vs. Recreational Drone Operations: Key Differences

The most important regulatory distinction in Costa Rica is between commercial drone operations and recreational hobby flights. If there is any form of compensation involved — direct payment, barter, or business benefit — the operation is considered commercial and requires full DGAC authorization.

Commercial operations subject to full DGAC authorization include all professional drone services such as our LiDAR mapping, photogrammetry, and GPS geopositioning surveys:

  • Drone surveying and mapping for any purpose (topography, construction, agriculture, real estate)
  • Aerial photography or videography performed for clients or business purposes
  • Construction site monitoring contracted to a developer or engineer
  • Agricultural analysis, crop health mapping, or precision agriculture services
  • Environmental monitoring for EIA submissions or SINAC compliance
  • Infrastructure inspection (power lines, pipelines, bridges, roads)
  • Any UAS operation where the operator receives payment or business benefit

Recreational flights by hobbyists on their own private property with drones under 25 kg may qualify for a simplified registration process, but even then, the same restricted zone rules apply. Anyone planning to fly commercially in Costa Rica without proper DGAC authorization faces fines, equipment confiscation, and potential criminal liability under aviation law.

⚠ Important: “Tourist Drone” Misconception

A common mistake among foreign property investors or developers is believing that a consumer drone brought from abroad can be operated commercially under tourist status. This is incorrect. Costa Rica’s aviation law applies regardless of nationality or the purpose of visit. Any commercial drone operation on Costa Rican soil requires DGAC authorization. Equipment brought in without proper import documentation can also be detained at customs.

DGAC Permit Requirements for Commercial Drone Operations

Obtaining commercial drone authorization in Costa Rica involves several components that must be in place before flight. Professional operators maintain these authorizations on an ongoing basis rather than applying per-project. Here is what the full commercial authorization framework requires:

Operator Certification

The drone pilot must hold a valid DGAC UAS operator certification. This involves demonstrating theoretical knowledge of aviation regulations, airspace management, meteorology, and drone systems, as well as practical competency assessment. Certifications are renewable and must be kept current. The operator is personally responsible for maintaining currency and complying with all conditions of their certification.

Aircraft Registration

Every drone used for commercial operations in Costa Rica must be registered with the DGAC. Registration includes a technical inspection of the aircraft, documentation of make, model, serial number, and maximum take-off weight. Each registered aircraft receives a DGAC registration number that must be displayed on the airframe. Drones over 25 kg require additional structural and safety assessments.

Liability Insurance

Commercial drone operators in Costa Rica are required to carry third-party liability insurance covering property damage and personal injury resulting from drone operations. The required coverage amounts vary based on the weight of the drone and the nature of the operation. Operations over populated areas or near critical infrastructure typically require higher coverage limits. Operators must be able to present proof of insurance upon request by DGAC or law enforcement.

Flight Plan Filing and Airspace Authorization

Before each commercial operation, a flight plan must be filed with the DGAC and/or the relevant air traffic control authority depending on the airspace class. For operations near controlled airspace — within 8 km of an airport or aerodrome — specific airspace authorization (often called a NOTAM or ATC coordination) is required before flight. For standard uncontrolled airspace at low altitude, the filing process is simpler but still mandatory.

Restricted and Prohibited Zones in Costa Rica

Even with full DGAC operator authorization, certain areas in Costa Rica impose additional restrictions or outright prohibitions on drone flight. Understanding these zones is critical for project planning, particularly for landowners in areas that frequently intersect restricted airspace.

Airport and Aerodrome Exclusion Zones

The most commonly encountered restriction is the 8 km radius around airports and aerodromes. Costa Rica has two international airports — Juan Santamaría (SJO) in Alajuela and Daniel Oduber (LIR) in Liberia — plus numerous domestic aerodromes serving the country’s many remote regions. The 8 km exclusion zone does not mean drone operations are impossible within these areas; it means that each flight requires specific coordination with the airport’s air traffic control, altitude is typically limited to 50 meters AGL without additional approval, and the authorization process is more involved.

For survey projects on properties within 8 km of an aerodrome — which is common given how many Costa Rican towns have small airstrips — our team initiates the ATC coordination process as soon as the project is confirmed. This adds time to scheduling but does not prevent the survey from occurring.

National Parks and SINAC Protected Areas

Costa Rica’s extensive system of national parks, biological reserves, wildlife refuges, and wetlands managed by SINAC present a dual-permit requirement. Flying over or within these protected areas requires both DGAC authorization and a separate permit from SINAC/MINAE. The SINAC permit process involves demonstrating that the drone operation serves a legitimate scientific, environmental, or authorized development purpose and that it will not disturb wildlife, nesting areas, or protected ecosystems.

This is particularly relevant for environmental impact assessments (EIAs), biodiversity studies, and surveys of properties adjacent to protected areas — all common requests in Costa Rica. Our team has established working relationships with SINAC regional offices to facilitate these permits efficiently. See our guide on drone surveys for EIA filings in Costa Rica for more detail on this process.

Urban Areas and Populated Zones

Drone operations above populated urban areas — defined as areas with concentrated residential or commercial development — require specific DGAC authorization beyond the standard commercial authorization. The primary concern is safety in the event of a technical failure. Operations over crowds, outdoor markets, sporting events, or densely populated neighborhoods are prohibited without explicit event-specific permits. Most survey work in urban areas involves property boundaries, rooftop inspections, or construction monitoring, where flight paths can be planned to avoid overflight of uninvolved people.

Military Zones and Critical Infrastructure

Costa Rica maintains several military and security-sensitive zones where drone operations are prohibited without special authorization from the Ministry of Security. Critical infrastructure including hydroelectric dams, power substations, water treatment facilities, and government buildings are also restricted. These restrictions rarely affect commercial survey work but are important context for any operator planning flights in areas they are unfamiliar with.

Altitude Rules and Visual Line of Sight Requirements

Two operational rules apply to virtually every drone flight in Costa Rica regardless of the specific location:

Maximum altitude: The standard ceiling for drone operations in uncontrolled airspace is 400 feet (approximately 120 meters) above ground level (AGL). This limit applies to most survey operations and is sufficient for photogrammetry, LiDAR mapping, and aerial photography at standard resolution. For large-area surveys requiring higher altitudes — some LiDAR missions benefit from flying at 150–200 meters for efficiency — a site-specific DGAC authorization is required. To understand how altitude and technology choice affect your project, read our complete aerial survey guide.

Visual Line of Sight (VLOS): All commercial drone operations in Costa Rica must be conducted within the pilot’s unaided visual line of sight. Extended visual line of sight (EVLOS) and beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS) operations require special DGAC authorization and are evaluated on a case-by-case basis. For large survey areas, we use multiple ground crew positions and planned flight grid patterns to maintain compliance with VLOS rules while covering extensive areas efficiently.

Why Hiring a Licensed Operator Protects Your Project

Some clients initially consider hiring informal or uncertified drone operators to save on cost. In Costa Rica’s regulatory environment, this creates significant legal and practical risk:

  • Data legality: Survey data collected without proper DGAC authorization may be inadmissible for permitting, EIA filings, or legal proceedings. SETENA and municipal authorities increasingly require documentation that aerial data was collected by licensed operators.
  • Liability exposure: If an unlicensed operator causes property damage or injury, the property owner or project client may share legal liability. Licensed operators carry the required insurance coverage that protects all parties.
  • Insurance claims: Construction and development insurance policies may include exclusions for damage caused by or related to unlicensed contractor work — which could include illegal drone operations.
  • Equipment confiscation: DGAC has the authority to confiscate drone equipment operated without authorization. This can halt a project entirely if a client brought their own equipment.

2026 Regulatory Updates: What’s New

Costa Rica’s DGAC has been progressively tightening its UAS regulatory framework in alignment with ICAO recommendations and broader Latin American aviation authority harmonization. Key developments relevant to commercial operators in 2026 include:

  • Electronic Conspicuity Requirements: Commercial drones operating in certain airspace classes are increasingly expected to broadcast ADS-B or Remote ID signals, allowing air traffic controllers to track UAS positions in real time. Operators should verify their equipment’s compatibility with current DGAC electronic visibility standards.
  • Stricter Urban Overflight Standards: Following increased drone activity over the San José metropolitan area, DGAC has tightened the documentation requirements for commercial flights over urban zones, particularly in densely populated cantons of Alajuela, San José, and Heredia.
  • Insurance Minimums Under Review: DGAC is reviewing liability insurance minimum requirements for commercial operators, with expected increases in required coverage for operations over populated areas. Our insurance coverage already exceeds current minimums to accommodate anticipated changes.
  • Bilateral Agreements: Costa Rica has been pursuing bilateral airspace coordination agreements with neighboring countries, relevant for cross-border infrastructure and environmental survey projects in border regions.

We monitor DGAC regulatory updates continuously and adapt our operational procedures accordingly. Clients are never responsible for tracking these changes — that is part of what professional operator authorization means.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to fly a drone commercially in Costa Rica?

Yes. Any commercial drone operation in Costa Rica requires authorization from the DGAC. This includes drone surveys, aerial photography for compensation, construction monitoring, agricultural mapping, and any other paid or commercial use. Operators must hold a valid DGAC UAS operator authorization, carry liability insurance, and file flight plans for operations near controlled airspace.

What are the altitude limits for drones in Costa Rica?

The standard maximum altitude is 400 feet (approximately 120 meters) above ground level for most uncontrolled airspace. Within 8 km of airports, operations require DGAC coordination and typically cannot exceed 50 meters AGL without a site-specific authorization. Operations above 120 meters AGL in urban areas also require special DGAC approval.

Where are drones restricted or prohibited in Costa Rica?

Restricted zones include: within 8 km of Juan Santamaría and Liberia international airports and all domestic aerodromes; over national parks and SINAC-managed protected areas without dual DGAC/SINAC permits; over populated urban areas without prior authorization; within military restricted zones; and over critical infrastructure. Many Costa Rican survey sites fall near at least one of these categories, which is why professional permit management is essential.

How long does it take to get a DGAC drone permit in Costa Rica?

Standard DGAC flight authorization processing takes 5 to 15 business days. For sites near airports or protected areas, the dual-permit process with ATC or SINAC can add 1 to 3 additional weeks.
