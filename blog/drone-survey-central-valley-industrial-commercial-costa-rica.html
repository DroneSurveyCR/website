The Central Valley Is Where Costa Rica Builds

Ringed by mountains and centred on San José, the Central Valley (Valle Central) is the country's economic heartland — home to the majority of its population, its universities, its government, and, increasingly, its industry. Heredia and Alajuela host the free-trade zones (zonas francas) where medical-device, electronics, and services companies cluster; Cartago carries a long manufacturing tradition; and the corridor around Juan Santamaría International Airport has become the natural home for warehousing, distribution, and logistics. When a multinational needs a new plant, a developer plans an industrial park, or a logistics operator wants a distribution centre, the Central Valley is usually where it lands.

But the same geography that makes the region attractive also makes it hard to build on. The valley floor is steep, folded, and cut by ravines (quebradas), and most flat ground was spoken for long ago. Industrial and commercial projects here almost always involve serious earthwork — cutting hillsides, filling gullies, and shaping level platforms for slabs and yards — and that work is only as good as the site data it is planned from. A drone survey gives developers and contractors that data fast: the entire parcel mapped to ±2cm, with the terrain, drainage, and volumes you need to design and cost the build before a single machine moves.

Why it matters here: On a hilly Central Valley industrial site, the difference between a good topographic model and a rough one is measured in truckloads of fill and weeks of schedule. Accurate, current, whole-of-site data lets you optimise cut-and-fill, design drainage that works, and reconcile what contractors actually moved — turning guesswork into measurement.

Case Study: Platforming a Logistics Park Near the Alajuela Airport Corridor

A developer building a distribution and light-industrial park on a sloping parcel in the Alajuela corridor came to us with a familiar Central Valley problem: the land looked buildable, but nobody could say with confidence how much earth would have to move to create the level platforms the warehouses needed. Their preliminary budget rested on a ground survey of a few dozen spot points and an experienced eye — enough to start a conversation, not enough to sign a contract. On a hillside site, an earthwork estimate that is off by even ten percent can swing the numbers by hundreds of truckloads of fill.

We mapped the full parcel in a single mobilisation, setting RTK ground control on stable points around the site. Within 72 hours the developer had a high-resolution orthophoto, a detailed digital terrain model, contours, and — most importantly — cut-and-fill volumes calculated against their proposed platform grades. As grading began we returned to re-fly the site, comparing each new surface against the baseline to track exactly how much material had been moved.

The outcome: The volume model let the developer lock a realistic earthwork budget before breaking ground, rework the platform layout to balance cut against fill and cut haul-off costs, and later reconcile the earthworks contractor's invoices against a measured record of what had actually been moved. What had been an anxious estimate became a defensible number the whole team could plan and pay against.

This is the recurring value of surveying an industrial site from the air: the data is objective, it is repeatable, and it serves feasibility, design, budgeting, and contract administration at once. Costa Rica DGAC Drone Regulations in the Central Valley

All commercial drone operations in Costa Rica are regulated by the Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC), and industrial site work is no exception. This matters especially in the Central Valley, where much of the region's most valuable industrial land sits close to controlled airspace. Data gathered by an unlicensed operator can be challenged in a permit or contract dispute, and an incident near an airport or a busy industrial zone creates real liability. Our operations comply with current DGAC requirements, which apply consistently across every region we serve:

Registered aircraft and certified remote pilots · operations kept within the standard altitude ceiling unless specific authorisation is held · respect for controlled and restricted airspace · and coordination of any authorisations required for the flight. Full details are in our 2026 DGAC drone regulations guide.

The Central Valley adds one dominant consideration: the airspace around Juan Santamaría International Airport (SJO) in Alajuela and the smaller Tobías Bolaños airport in Pavas is controlled, and a great deal of the region's warehousing and industrial development sits directly under those approach and departure corridors. Flights in these areas are planned and cleared with DGAC in advance, and in some zones they require specific altitude restrictions or authorisation. We handle that coordination as part of the job — so wherever a site falls, the survey is flown legally, safely, and without holding up your project schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions — Central Valley Industrial & Commercial Drone Surveys

Why use a drone survey for an industrial or commercial site in the Central Valley?

Industrial and commercial projects live or die on accurate site data, and the Central Valley's steep, uneven terrain makes that data expensive to gather on the ground. A drone maps an entire industrial parcel — topography, existing structures, drainage lines, and access roads — to ±2cm in a single flight, then delivers earthwork volumes, contours, and an orthophoto within 72 hours. That lets developers of free-trade zones, industrial parks, warehouses, and logistics hubs cut-and-fill efficiently, avoid design surprises, and keep the whole build team working from one current dataset instead of a months-old paper survey.

Can a drone survey calculate cut-and-fill earthwork volumes for a warehouse or logistics pad?

Yes — this is one of the highest-value uses of a drone on an industrial site. From the surface model we calculate exactly how many cubic metres of material must be cut or filled to reach your design platform level, and we can re-fly the site as grading proceeds to track how much has actually been moved. On a large logistics pad or industrial park that means you can reconcile contractor haul claims against measured reality, forecast material costs before you break ground, and confirm the platform is at design grade before the slab goes down.

Do drone surveys help with free-trade zone and municipal permitting in Costa Rica?

They do. A survey-grade orthophoto, digital terrain model, and contour set give your engineers and architects the base data they need for site plans, drainage design, and the documentation that municipalities and SETENA environmental review expect. For free-trade zone (régimen de zona franca) developments and industrial parks, having accurate, dated terrain and boundary data up front smooths the approval process and reduces the risk of costly redesigns once construction is underway.

How does a drone survey handle the Central Valley's hilly, built-up terrain?

The Central Valley combines steep slopes, ravines (quebradas), and dense existing development, which is difficult and slow for a ground crew but ideal for aerial mapping. We fly with RTK GPS ground control for ±2cm accuracy, and where a site is partly covered by trees or vegetation we add LiDAR to capture the true ground surface beneath the canopy. Airspace around Juan Santamaría International Airport in Alajuela is controlled, so flights there are planned and cleared with DGAC in advance.
