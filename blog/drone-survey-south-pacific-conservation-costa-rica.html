The South Pacific Is Costa Rica's Living Laboratory

The Osa Peninsula and the wider South Pacific hold what National Geographic once called "the most biologically intense place on Earth." Corcovado National Park anchors a landscape of primary rainforest, mangrove estuaries along Golfo Dulce, and a patchwork of private reserves, reforestation plots, and eco-lodges stretching from Puerto Jiménez to Drake Bay and up the coast through Uvita and Ojochal. It is a region defined not by how fast it builds, but by how carefully it protects — and that creates a very specific surveying problem.

Conservation needs measurement. To prove that a forest is regenerating, that a carbon project is sequestering what it claims, or that a new eco-lodge sits lightly on its land, you need accurate, repeatable data across terrain that is steep, stream-cut, and often hidden under a closed canopy a ground crew simply cannot walk through. Traditional ground survey samples a few plots slowly and leaves most of a property unmeasured; satellite imagery is too coarse and too cloud-blocked to track growth at the scale that matters. A drone LiDAR survey closes that gap — turning a whole reserve into precise, dated, measurable data without clearing a single sightline. How Drone Surveys Help South Pacific Conservation Work

Conservation surveying is repeat-business mapping: the same forest flown on a schedule so the data tells a story of growth and change over time. Each flight is captured with RTK GPS ground control so every dataset locks to the same real-world coordinates, which is what makes flight-to-flight comparison trustworthy. From those flights we produce the measurements and records that drive decisions on a reserve, a reforestation plot, or an eco-development. Why LiDAR — not photogrammetry — for the rainforest

This is the one region where sensor choice is not a preference but a requirement. A camera-only photogrammetry survey can only map what it can see, and over closed primary forest that means the top of the canopy and nothing below it. LiDAR fires hundreds of thousands of laser pulses per second; enough of them slip through gaps in the leaves to reach the forest floor and return, so by keeping the last returns we reconstruct the true ground surface beneath even the densest Osa canopy. The full point cloud simultaneously captures canopy height and structure. That dual view — ground and canopy from a single flight — is exactly what conservation, reforestation, and carbon work depend on, which is why we fly LiDAR for almost every South Pacific project.

Case Study: Baselining a Reforestation Reserve Near Puerto Jiménez

A private reserve on the Golfo Dulce side of the Osa Peninsula, mixing recovering pasture with patches of mature forest, came to us with a problem familiar to anyone running a reforestation or restoration project: they were planting and protecting tens of hectares, reporting to international donors, and enrolling parcels in Costa Rica's payments-for-ecosystem-services program — but their evidence of progress was a scatter of hand-measured ground plots that covered a tiny fraction of the land and were brutal to collect on steep, stream-cut terrain.

We flew the entire property with drone LiDAR, establishing permanent RTK ground control and capturing a baseline in a single mobilisation. Processing within 72 hours produced a bare-earth terrain model, a canopy height model, and a high-resolution orthophoto covering every hectare — the recovering pasture, the planted blocks, and the standing forest alike. We delivered the data in formats their GIS team could drop straight into QGIS.

The outcome: For the first time the reserve had a wall-to-wall, dated baseline of canopy height and cover across the whole property, not a sample. A return flight the following year measured growth and survival block by block — objective evidence for donor reporting and PSA documentation that no longer depended on extrapolating from a few plots. The terrain model also flagged erosion-prone drainages to prioritise for planting, turning the survey into a planning tool as well as a monitoring record.

This is the recurring value of mapping conservation land from the air: the data is objective, it is repeatable, and it serves the whole effort — planning, reporting, and verification — at once. Costa Rica DGAC Drone Regulations — Plus SINAC for Protected Areas

All commercial drone operations in Costa Rica are regulated by the Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC). For conservation work this is not a formality: data gathered by an unlicensed operator can be challenged in a permit or certification process, and an incident in sensitive habitat creates real liability. Our operations comply with current DGAC requirements, which apply consistently across every region we serve:

Registered aircraft and certified remote pilots · operations kept within the standard altitude ceiling unless specific authorisation is held · respect for controlled and restricted airspace · and coordination of any authorisations required for the flight. Full details are in our 2026 DGAC drone regulations guide.

The South Pacific adds one layer no other region does: protected areas. Flying over a national park or protected wildlife area — Corcovado, Piedras Blancas, the Térraba-Sierpe wetlands, and others — requires authorisation from SINAC, the national protected-areas authority, on top of standard DGAC compliance. Most of our conservation work is on private reserves and farms adjoining protected land, where DGAC compliance is enough, but whenever a survey touches a protected area we coordinate the necessary SINAC permissions first, plan flights to avoid disturbing nesting and wildlife, and gather the data legally and respectfully.

Frequently Asked Questions — South Pacific Conservation Drone Surveys

Can a drone really map the forest floor under thick Osa Peninsula canopy?

Yes — but only with LiDAR. A LiDAR sensor fires hundreds of thousands of laser pulses per second, and a portion of those pulses slip through gaps in the canopy to strike the ground and return. By keeping only the last returns we reconstruct a bare-earth terrain model beneath even the dense primary forest of the Osa Peninsula, while the full point cloud also gives canopy height and structure. A camera-only photogrammetry survey cannot see through closed canopy and only maps the treetops, which is why we use LiDAR for conservation and reforestation work in the South Pacific.

How do drone surveys support reforestation and carbon projects?

Drone LiDAR establishes a precise, dated baseline of canopy height, tree count, and forest structure across a property, then later flights are compared against it to measure growth, survival, and regeneration objectively. That repeatable, geo-referenced record supports carbon-project monitoring, reporting, and verification, payments-for-ecosystem-services (PSA) documentation, and grant or donor reporting — replacing slow, sampled ground plots with wall-to-wall measurement of the whole site.

Do you need special permission to fly a drone over Corcovado or other protected areas?

Yes. Flying over national parks and protected wildlife areas requires authorisation from SINAC (Costa Rica's national protected-areas authority) in addition to standard DGAC compliance for the flight itself. Much of our South Pacific conservation work is on private reserves and farms adjoining protected land, where DGAC compliance is sufficient, but whenever a flight touches a protected area we coordinate the necessary SINAC permissions first so the survey is gathered legally and respects wildlife.

What can a drone survey tell me before I build an eco-lodge or reserve in Puerto Jiménez?

A drone survey gives you an accurate terrain model, slope and drainage analysis, canopy map, and high-resolution orthophoto of the whole property before you commit to a footprint. On the steep, forested, stream-cut land typical around Puerto Jiménez and Golfo Dulce, that lets you site cabins, trails, and access roads to minimise tree loss and earthwork, plan drainage correctly, and document existing forest cover for permits and conservation easements — all without clearing sightlines for a ground crew.

Larger reserves and farms are quoted at $80 per hectare, and recurring monitoring programs for reforestation or carbon projects are priced per visit at a reduced rate once control is established.
