How Much Does It Cost to Bring a Drone Into Costa Rica? (2026 Guide)

Published: June 16, 2026 8 min read Category: Regulations

It's the question every traveler, real-estate buyer, and project manager asks before flying down with their drone: what will it actually cost to get it through Costa Rican customs and into the air? The short answer is that most people pay nothing at the border — but a high-value rig, a commercial mission, or a missed declaration can turn a free carry-on into a tax bill of hundreds of dollars. Here is the complete 2026 breakdown of customs costs, import taxes, and DGAC registration so there are no surprises at San José or Liberia airport.

The Short Answer: Most Drones Enter Free

Costa Rica places no outright ban on bringing drones into the country. If you arrive as a tourist with a single consumer drone — a DJI Mini, Air, or Mavic packed in your carry-on — you will almost always walk through customs without paying a colón. Personal-use electronics that you bring in and take home with you are treated as part of your luggage, not as a commercial import.

The cost only appears in three situations: when the gear's value crosses the customs declaration threshold, when customs decides your purpose is commercial, or when your drone is heavy or used in a way that triggers DGAC registration requirements. Understanding each of those triggers is the key to keeping your entry free — and to budgeting accurately if it won't be.

Customs Costs: The $500 Threshold and Form D-151

Costa Rican customs generally expect travelers to declare imported goods valued above roughly USD $500 per item. A consumer drone usually sits below or near that line, which is why most tourists pass through untaxed. But a professional rig — a DJI Matrice, an enterprise mapping platform, or a drone bundled with extra batteries, a high-end camera, and a controller — can easily exceed it.

When the value is over the threshold, or when an officer suspects the equipment is for resale or commercial work, you may be asked to complete Form D-151 and clear the item formally. At that point import charges apply, and they add up faster than most people expect.

Charge Approximate Rate Applies To
Import duty ~13% Declared customs value
VAT (IVA) 13% Value plus duty

Effective total ~25–28% Of the drone's value

In practice that means a $1,500 commercial drone could carry roughly $375–$420 in combined taxes if it is formally imported. A $4,000 enterprise mapping platform could face $1,000 or more. These figures are estimates — exact duties depend on tariff classification and the declared value — but they show why the line between "personal carry-on" and "commercial import" matters so much to your budget.

How to keep your entry free

  • Travel with a single, clearly personal-use drone — not multiples.
  • Carry it used and unboxed; brand-new, sealed retail packaging signals resale.
  • Keep the receipt or know the current resale value in case you're asked.
  • Be honest if questioned — recreational use under the threshold is rarely taxed.
  • For genuine commercial gear, budget the ~28% rather than risk penalties.

DGAC Registration: When Your Drone Needs Papers

Customs is only half the story. Once your drone is in the country, the Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC) governs whether you can legally fly it. The good news for travelers is that registration cost is usually zero, because most recreational drones fall below the registration weight.

Recreational use (under 25 kg)

If you're flying a consumer drone weighing less than 25 kg for personal enjoyment, you generally do not need to register it or hold a pilot license. There's no registration fee for hobby flying. You simply have to follow the country's general operating rules, which we cover below. This is the case for virtually every tourist drone on the market.

Drones over 25 kg

Registration with the DGAC is mandatory for any drone weighing more than 25 kg. Very few travelers carry aircraft this large, but heavy-lift cinematography and industrial platforms do cross the line.

Commercial operations

This is the most commonly misunderstood point. If you intend to use your drone to earn money in Costa Rica — paid real-estate photography, mapping, surveying, inspections, or any client work — the operation must be registered with the DGAC regardless of the drone's weight. Commercial authorization involves paperwork, proof of competency, and often insurance, and it is not something you can arrange overnight at the airport.

The Hidden Cost: Operating Rules and Restricted Zones

Even a free, registration-exempt drone carries a cost if you break the rules — fines, confiscation, or a ruined trip. Costa Rica's operating rules are straightforward but strictly enforced, especially near airports and protected areas:

  • Daylight and good weather only — no night flying without authorization.
  • Visual line of sight at all times — keep the drone in view.
  • Maximum altitude of 120 metres (about 400 ft) above ground level unless specifically cleared higher.
  • Minimum 30 metres from people, animals, and buildings not part of your operation.
  • No flying over national parks and many protected areas without explicit permission — a major restriction given how much of Costa Rica is conservation land.

That last point catches many visitors off guard. Much of what makes Costa Rica worth filming — Manuel Antonio, Corcovado, Arenal, Tortuguero — sits inside protected zones where casual drone flying is prohibited. If your goal is professional aerial data over sensitive terrain, working with a local operator who already holds the right permissions saves both money and legal risk. Tourist vs. Professional: Which Path Is Cheaper?

For a hobbyist capturing vacation footage, bringing your own drone is clearly the cheapest option — likely free at the border and free to register. The cost calculus flips entirely when the work is commercial. Between potential import tax of ~28%, mandatory DGAC commercial registration, insurance, and the time spent securing permissions for restricted areas, importing and operating your own drone for paid work in Costa Rica can become a significant, slow investment.

If you need deliverables rather than a hobby flight — topographic maps, boundary verification, LiDAR canopy data, or construction monitoring — hiring an established local provider is almost always faster and more economical than navigating customs and DGAC yourself. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I have to pay tax to bring a drone into Costa Rica as a tourist?

Usually not. A single consumer drone carried for personal use and taken home with you is treated as luggage and typically enters free. Taxes generally apply only when the value exceeds the ~$500 declaration threshold or customs determines the use is commercial.

Q: How much is the import tax on a drone in Costa Rica?

If a drone is formally imported, expect a combined rate of roughly 25–28% of its value — approximately 13% import duty, 13% VAT, and around 1% in additional levies. A $1,500 drone could therefore carry $375–$420 in taxes.

Q: Do I need to register my drone with the DGAC?

Recreational drones under 25 kg do not require registration or a license. Registration is mandatory for drones over 25 kg and for any commercial operation, regardless of weight.

Q: Can I fly my drone commercially while visiting Costa Rica?

Only with proper DGAC authorization. Any paid work — photography, mapping, surveying, inspections — must be registered as a commercial operation, which requires advance paperwork and often insurance. For client-ready results, hiring a locally licensed operator is usually faster and cheaper.

Q: Where am I not allowed to fly a drone in Costa Rica?

National parks and many protected areas are off-limits without explicit permission, and you must stay clear of airports, keep below 120 metres, maintain visual line of sight, and stay 30 metres from people and buildings. Much of Costa Rica's most scenic terrain is protected land.

The Bottom Line

For the vast majority of visitors, bringing a drone into Costa Rica in 2026 costs nothing: a personal-use consumer drone under 25 kg enters duty-free and needs no registration to fly recreationally. Costs appear only when the gear is high-value (potentially ~28% import tax over the $500 threshold), heavy (DGAC registration above 25 kg), or used commercially (mandatory DGAC authorization plus paperwork and insurance).
