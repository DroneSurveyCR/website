Drone Surveys for Solar Farm Site Assessment in Costa Rica

Costa Rica already runs on close to 100% renewable electricity, and solar is the fastest-growing piece of that mix — from utility-scale plants feeding the grid to the private industrial and agricultural arrays that developers are now building to hedge against tariffs. Every one of those projects lives or dies on a single question that gets asked far too late: does the land actually suit the panels? A parcel that looks flat and open from the road can hide a slope that throws morning shadows across half the array, a drainage line that will flood the inverter pads every green season, or grading costs that quietly erase the project's margin.

A drone survey answers that question up front, and cheaply. In a single morning's flight it captures a centimetre-accurate three-dimensional model of the whole site — the ground, the vegetation, the surrounding tree lines and structures — that the design and finance teams then use to optimise the layout, forecast energy yield, plan drainage and price the earthworks. This guide walks through exactly how that works for a solar project in Costa Rica: what the survey measures, what you receive, how it carries through from feasibility to operations, and what it costs. Why the terrain model decides a solar project

The economics of a photovoltaic (PV) farm are unusually sensitive to topography. Panels are mounted in long rows, and the spacing between those rows — the pitch — is set to stop one row shading the next through the year. On a perfectly flat site that pitch is a simple calculation. On sloped ground it changes row by row, because a south-facing slope lets you pack rows tighter while a north-facing slope forces them apart, costing you usable area. Get the terrain model wrong and you either lose generating capacity or build in self-shading losses that no amount of good hardware will recover.

That is why the foundation of any solar assessment is a bare-earth digital terrain model (DTM) and the slope and aspect maps derived from it. The DTM is the ground surface with vegetation and structures stripped away — the same deliverable a civil engineer designs a road or a building pad on, and the one we describe in full in our guide to drone survey deliverables. From it, the design team extracts the slope of every part of the site and its aspect (the compass direction the ground faces), then places and tilts the mounting structures to match. That single dataset drives the panel count, the racking design, and ultimately the megawatt-hours the plant is financed against.

Shading and horizon analysis: protecting the yield forecast

The most expensive mistakes on a solar site are shading mistakes, because they are invisible until the sun is in the wrong place. This is where the drone survey earns its cost several times over. Because the flight produces a complete digital surface model (DSM) — the terrain plus every tree, ridge and building on and around the site — that model can be loaded into design software and used to simulate the sun's path across the sky for any hour of any month.

Run that simulation and you can see precisely where the morning and afternoon shadows fall, how much annual production a particular tree line or hillside will cost, and which vegetation needs clearing before it is worth putting panels behind it. On Costa Rica's terrain, where sites are frequently ringed by fast-growing tropical forest and backed by steep ridges, the horizon can steal a surprising amount of early and late-day generation. Modelling it from an accurate survey — rather than eyeballing it on a site visit — is the difference between a yield forecast a lender will trust and an optimistic guess that unravels after commissioning. It is the same 3D surface data that powers the elevation work in our topographic survey service, applied to the specific problem of where the light goes.

Drainage, earthworks and grading in a tropical climate

Two things make Costa Rica harder on solar sites than a dry climate: rainfall and slope. The green season delivers intense, concentrated downpours, and water that is not deliberately routed away will pond around foundations, erode access roads and undermine the array. The survey's terrain model is exactly what a drainage engineer needs to trace how water moves across the site, position swales and culverts, and keep the critical equipment — inverter stations, transformer pads, cable trenches — clear of the flow paths.

The same model prices the dirt. By comparing the existing ground surface against the graded design surface, the survey produces cut-and-fill volume estimates — how many cubic metres of earth have to be moved to create the pads and level the racking rows. Earthworks are one of the largest and most variable line items in a solar build, and having a real number before machines mobilise is what keeps the budget honest. It is the same volumetric technique behind our stockpile volume measurement work, pointed at grading instead of aggregate. Where the parcel is heavily vegetated — common on Costa Rican land that has not yet been cleared — we fly LiDAR rather than photogrammetry, because only LiDAR reliably reads the ground through the canopy; our LiDAR vs photogrammetry guide explains when that choice matters.

From feasibility to construction to operations

A drone survey is not a one-time feasibility exercise; it earns its place across the whole project life cycle. At feasibility and design, the orthomosaic, DTM, DSM and shading model do the heavy lifting described above, and the accuracy of it all rests on proper ground control — the centimetre-level positioning we cover in our survey accuracy guide, which is what lets the design team trust the numbers.

During construction, repeat flights track progress against the plan: are the pads graded to spec, are the racking rows going in on the surveyed positions, is the earthwork volume matching the estimate? This is the same construction-monitoring cadence we run on building sites across the country, described in our construction drone survey work — an aerial record on a schedule, so disputes and overruns are caught early rather than at handover.

Once the plant is operational, the survey work shifts from mapping to inspection. A drone carrying a radiometric thermal camera is flown low over the finished array, and because faulty cells, failed bypass diodes, disconnected strings and soiling all run hotter than healthy modules, they show up instantly as temperature anomalies on the thermal image. A single flight can scan tens of thousands of panels in the time a technician would need to check a few hundred by hand, and the output is a georeferenced map that sends the maintenance crew straight to the modules that are dragging down output. For a plant whose revenue is measured in generation, finding a dead string in a day instead of a quarter is a direct return.

The Costa Rica specifics: permits, environment and access

Two local realities shape solar surveying here. The first is regulatory. Commercial drone flights are governed by the DGAC, and larger or utility-scale developments frequently trigger an environmental review through SETENA — the kind of baseline site documentation that aerial mapping supports well. We keep every flight compliant with current rules, which we track in our DGAC drone regulations guide, and the orthomosaic and terrain data double as clean evidence for the environmental impact assessment that a project of any size will need to clear.

The second is access. Good solar land in Costa Rica is often in Guanacaste's dry northwest or on rolling parcels away from the towns — ground that is slow, hot and sometimes impassable for a walking survey crew, but trivial for a drone to cover in a single flight. That access advantage is a large part of why aerial survey is not just more precise but genuinely cheaper than a ground crew on this terrain, a comparison we lay out in full in our drone vs ground survey guide.

What you receive, and what it costs

For a solar site assessment we typically deliver an orthomosaic of the whole parcel, a bare-earth DTM and a full DSM, slope and aspect maps, a horizon and shading analysis, contour lines at your chosen interval, a classified point cloud for the design team, and cut-and-fill volumes against a proposed grading surface — supplied as GeoTIFF, LAS/LAZ and DWG/DXF so they open directly in whatever software your engineers use. For operational plants, the deliverable is a georeferenced thermal anomaly map of the array.

Cost scales with the site area, the terrain and how much of that package you need. A feasibility-stage topographic survey of a small-to-medium parcel is usually a few hundred to low-thousands of US dollars; a large utility-scale site with LiDAR under vegetation and a complete design deliverable set costs more, and thermal inspections of an operating plant are generally priced per megawatt or per flight. Frequently Asked Questions About Solar Farm Drone Surveys

Why do you need a drone survey for a solar farm?

A solar farm's yield and its construction cost are both set by the shape of the land. A drone survey delivers a centimetre-accurate terrain model that lets engineers optimise row spacing and tilt, model exactly how much shading the terrain and horizon will cast, plan drainage so tropical rainfall does not undermine the array, and calculate cut-and-fill earthworks before machines mobilise. On Costa Rica's sloped, vegetated sites that data is far cheaper from the air than with a ground crew — and it protects the energy model the project is financed against.

What does a solar site drone survey deliver?

A typical assessment package includes an orthomosaic, a bare-earth DTM and a DSM that captures existing vegetation and structures, slope and aspect maps, a horizon and shading analysis, contours, a classified point cloud, and cut-and-fill volumes for the grading plan. For operational plants we also fly thermal inspections that reveal underperforming or faulty panels across the array. See our deliverables guide for what each output is.

Can a drone find shading problems on a solar site?

Yes. Because the survey produces a full 3D surface model including terrain, tree lines and nearby structures, it can run a sun-path shading analysis for any time of day and month of the year. That shows where morning and afternoon shadows fall, how much annual production a given ridge or tree line will cost, and which vegetation to clear — catching it at assessment stage rather than after commissioning.

How is a drone used to inspect an existing solar plant?

An operating array is scanned with a radiometric thermal camera flown at low altitude. Faulty cells, failed diodes, disconnected strings and soiling all run hotter than healthy modules and appear as temperature anomalies invisible to the eye. One flight can cover tens of thousands of panels quickly and produce a georeferenced map that points crews straight to the modules that need attention.

A feasibility-stage topographic survey of a small-to-medium parcel is typically a few hundred to low-thousands of US dollars; a large utility-scale site with LiDAR under vegetation costs more, and thermal inspections are usually priced per megawatt or per flight.
