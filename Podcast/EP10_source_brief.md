## SOURCE MATERIAL (Conversational Podcast-Ready Tone)

Picture this. You're a developer grading a hillside lot in Alajuela, and the earthwork contractor just handed you an invoice for eighteen thousand five hundred cubic meters of cut material removed. You stare at it. How do you even check that? You weren't counting truck loads. The site looks lower than it did, sure, but is it eighteen thousand five hundred cubic meters lower, or is it actually less than that and you're about to overpay by twenty grand? That uncertainty — that "I have no way to verify what I'm paying for" feeling — is exactly the gap a construction drone survey closes. And today we're getting into how drones turn a fast-moving, expensive Costa Rica construction site into something you can actually measure, month over month, to the cubic meter.

So let's start with why construction here is special. Costa Rica is mountainous, which means almost every development involves serious earthmoving. Cut-and-fill on a hillside can move tens of thousands of cubic meters, and small errors in volume compound into big payment disputes. Add in remote sites — resorts, farm infrastructure, road projects hours from anywhere — plus two rainy seasons squeezing your timeline, plus SETENA environmental permitting that increasingly wants documented site conditions. All of that makes accurate, repeatable, fast site measurement more valuable here than almost anywhere else. A drone flies the whole site in a morning and hands you data a ground crew would take days to collect.

Now here's the headline application, the one that pays for the whole program: earthwork volume verification. When a contractor bills by the cubic meter, those quantities need to match what actually happened on the ground. A drone survey before and after any major earthmoving creates a precise before-and-after terrain comparison, and the volume difference is calculated to the cubic meter — not estimated from truck counts, but calculated from millions of real elevation measurements. And here's the kicker: owners routinely find five to fifteen percent discrepancies between contractor invoices and drone-calculated volumes.

Let me give you the real example, because this is where the listener feels it. That developer in Alajuela, twelve-hectare site, got billed for eighteen thousand five hundred cubic meters of cut removal. The drone survey showed the actual cut volume was sixteen thousand two hundred cubic meters. That's a twelve percent discrepancy — worth about twenty-two thousand dollars at the contracted rate. The drone survey cost one thousand nine hundred and sixty dollars. Net saving: over twenty thousand dollars. That's the entire survey program paying for itself many times over on a single flight.

But earthwork verification is just one of five big jobs. Second is monthly progress monitoring. Each flight produces a georeferenced orthophoto and an updated terrain model showing exactly how the site changed. That record is gold for lender drawdown requests, it lets absentee owners see their site from anywhere in the world, it's evidence if a dispute or insurance claim comes up, and it becomes a permanent construction archive. Third is cut-and-fill analysis against design — comparing the current site to the engineered design surface with color-coded difference maps that show over-excavation and under-excavation zones, delivered as a GeoTIFF overlay straight into AutoCAD Civil 3D. Fourth is as-built documentation — at completion, a georeferenced record of what was actually built versus designed, which municipal governments and MOPT increasingly require for sign-off. And fifth is plain old safety and site inspection — a bird's-eye view that catches slope stability concerns and drainage problems before they become incidents.

Let's talk about what lands in your inbox. Every construction survey includes a georeferenced orthophoto, a Digital Terrain Model in GeoTIFF and point cloud, an earthwork volume report as PDF and Excel, a color-coded cut-and-fill difference map, progress photography from multiple angles, a full LAS or LAZ point cloud for Civil 3D or Revit or Navisworks, and a documented accuracy report with check point residuals. And on accuracy — these surveys hit plus or minus one to three centimeters vertical using RTK-corrected GPS and LiDAR, which is more than enough for earthwork volumes, grade verification, and as-builts on virtually any project in the country.

Here's how a job actually runs. First, pre-survey coordination — we review your site plans, confirm whether it's a baseline, progress, or as-built survey, and check DGAC airspace requirements. Sites near Juan Santamaría or Liberia airports need DGAC flight authorization, which we handle; most sites are outside controlled airspace. Second, ground control setup — a GPS base station goes in, and on repeat visits we reoccupy the existing control marks so the before and after reference the exact same coordinate system. That consistency is critical for accurate volume change detection. Third, the flight — our DJI Matrice 300 RTK flies a programmed grid at sixty to eighty meters, and most sites under twenty hectares are done in a single morning. Fourth, processing. And fifth, delivery: deliverables land via secure download within seventy-two hours.

Now the money question — pricing. It's site-based and simple. Up to five hectares is a thousand dollars, then eighty dollars per additional hectare. Sites of five to fifteen hectares are that thousand plus the per-hectare add; fifteen to fifty runs roughly two thousand to thirty-six hundred. And if you commit to a monthly monitoring program of six or more surveys, that up-to-five-hectare rate drops to eight hundred a visit. Travel adds fifty to a hundred in the Greater Metropolitan Area, one-fifty to two-fifty for remote Guanacaste, Pacific, or Caribbean sites. Rush twenty-four-hour delivery is an extra two-fifty.

Who's this for across the country? Residential and mixed-use developments where lenders now request aerial progress reports. Road and highway infrastructure — linear sites are ideal, we cover an alignment of any length in a day for MOPT and municipal payment certification. Free Trade Zone construction around Alajuela, Heredia, and Cartago. Tourism and hospitality builds in Guanacaste and the coasts. And agricultural infrastructure scheduled around crop cycles. A common question: can we fly while construction is active? Yes — flight over a ten-hectare site is twenty to forty minutes, crews are back to full activity within an hour, and after rain the muddy texture actually improves photogrammetry results.

So if you're an owner, developer, contractor, or PM and you've ever stared at an earthwork invoice with no way to check it, this is the data that gives you a single source of truth.
