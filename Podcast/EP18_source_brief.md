## SOURCE MATERIAL (Conversational Podcast-Ready Tone)

Let me paint you a picture. You've found the property. The listing photo shows a clean rectangular lot, the seller hands you a plano catastrado — that's the registered cadastral map — and your attorney pulls the title at the National Registry and says it's clean. Everything looks ready to close. You're about to wire the deposit. And here's the one question almost nobody asks until it's too late: does the land on the map actually match the land on the ground? In Costa Rica, the honest answer is — surprisingly often — "not exactly." And today we're getting into how a single drone flight can catch that gap before it costs you tens of thousands of dollars.

So let's start with why this even happens, because it surprises people. Costa Rica runs two separate property systems. There's the Registro Nacional, which is the legal title — who owns it. And there's the Catastro Nacional, the cadastral map — the physical shape and area of the parcel, what's called the cabida. In theory those two line up perfectly. In practice, decades of imperfect surveying have left real gaps between them.

Why? A few reasons. First, old survey methods — a lot of these planos were drawn decades ago with chains and transits, or early GPS, sometimes with no real georeferencing to the national coordinate grid at all. The geometry is approximate. Second, lost or moved mojones — those are the physical boundary markers, stakes or concrete posts, and over the years they get buried by vegetation, washed out by erosion, removed during construction, or quietly relocated by a neighbor. Third, overlapping plans — traslapes — where neighboring parcels were surveyed independently at different times, and when you finally georeference them all, the registered boundaries actually overlap. Meaning two owners hold paper title to the same strip of land. Fourth, fences built by eye — walls and hedgerows placed by approximation, sometimes meters off the legal line. And fifth, informal family subdivisions divided over generations without anyone re-surveying.

Here's the kicker: none of this shows up in a title search. Your attorney can confirm the seller legally owns "Finca 123456" with a registered plano of two hectares — and be completely correct — while the land you actually walk is one-point-seven hectares, fenced into the neighbor's coffee plot, with one corner sitting inside a river setback. The legal record and the physical reality are two different things. Only a measurement on the ground reconciles them.

So how does a drone fix this? The whole job is: precisely measure the physical property, then compare it against the registered plano catastrado. Step one — RTK-corrected aerial mapping. The drone flies a programmed grid carrying an RTK GPS system and a high-res camera, or LiDAR if the lot is forested. RTK correction ties every single measurement to CRTM05 — that's the official Costa Rica coordinate system, the same datum the National Cadastre uses. On open terrain you get one-to-five centimeter horizontal accuracy. Step two — locating the mojones and physical features. The georeferenced orthophoto and 3D model let us pinpoint existing markers, fence lines, walls, structures, roads, rivers, tree lines, all to the centimeter. Where the mojones still exist, we capture their exact coordinates. Where they're missing, we show you where the registered corner should be relative to what's physically there. Step three — we overlay the registered plano catastrado right on top of the drone data. And that side-by-side instantly answers the questions: does the registered shape fit the physical lot? Do the corners land on the actual mojones? Is the area correct? Does the boundary cross a fence, a building, or a river nobody mentioned? Step four — you get a clear visual and numerical discrepancy report: the orthophoto with both the physical features and the registered boundary drawn on top, measured area versus titled area, and a list of any overlaps, encroachments, or access problems.

Now I want to be really straight about the legal line here, because it matters. In Costa Rica, the official plano catastrado that gets registered at the National Registry has to be prepared and signed by a licensed surveyor — a topógrafo — carrying a CFIA professional stamp. A drone survey does not replace that legal certification. What the drone survey does is two things really well: due diligence verification before you buy, and supplying fast, accurate, georeferenced data to the licensed surveyor if a new or corrected plano is needed. Think of it like the difference between a home inspection and a notarized deed. The drone survey tells you what you're really buying. The topógrafo's stamped plano is what gets registered. In practice they work together — we frequently fly a property so the buyer can see the boundary situation clearly and cheaply, and that centimeter-accurate dataset then feeds straight into the surveyor's formal process and saves them field time.

Let's talk about what this actually catches, because this is the part that saves real money. Area smaller than the title — the cabida discrepancy — which is a direct basis to renegotiate the price, because you're paying for hectares you won't receive. A neighbor's fence or building encroaching — that's a dispute you'd inherit at closing, with possible adverse-possession risk. A registered plan overlapping the adjacent parcel — the traslape — two owners, one strip of land, serious legal flag. No legal road access — a missing servidumbre — which makes the lot landlocked and can block permits, financing, and resale. Maritime Zone overlap — the first two hundred meters from the high-tide line is public or concession land, not titleable. River and stream setbacks — protected riparian buffers of ten to fifty meters that can erase a big chunk of buildable area. And steep-slope restrictions, where development on slopes over thirty to forty percent is limited. A few of these — setbacks, slope limits, forested terrain — need real elevation data, not just a flat outline, which is where a topographic survey and, under tree cover, LiDAR come in.

Where does this matter most? Honestly, in exactly the markets foreign buyers love most — remote, scenic, often forested, frequently bought sight-unseen. The South Pacific and Osa, with steep jungle ocean-view lots where you genuinely cannot judge the boundaries or buildable area by eye. The Guanacaste coast, where being near the beach means constant Maritime Zone overlap questions and tight setbacks. The Central Valley foothills, full of subdivided family land and hillside lots where the fence and the registered line almost never agree. And the Caribbean, Limón, with mixed plantation and forest parcels on old, low-precision plans.

So how do you actually fit this into a purchase? The sweet spot is the due diligence period — after you've got an accepted offer and the plano in hand, but before final closing. The sequence is simple. One, get the documents: the registered plano catastrado, the folio real, and ideally the planos of the neighboring parcels. Two, book the flight — send us the location and the plano, and we'll decide photogrammetry versus LiDAR based on tree cover. Three, survey day — we set up the RTK base station and fly it, usually thirty to ninety minutes on site for a typical lot. Four, overlay and report — we georeference to CRTM05, overlay the registered boundary, and deliver the orthophoto, area comparison, and discrepancy report, usually within three to five business days. Five, act on the findings — clean result, proceed with confidence; problems found, hand the data to your attorney and a surveyor to renegotiate, correct the plano, or walk away.

The bottom line is this. A clean title tells you the seller owns the parcel. A drone boundary survey tells you what that parcel actually is — its real shape, its real area, and every surprise hiding between the paperwork and the dirt. The survey is a rounding error against the price of the land, and a tiny fraction of what a boundary dispute, a landlocked lot, or a twenty-percent area shortfall would cost you after closing. Smart buyers confirm both before they wire a deposit.
