## SOURCE MATERIAL (Conversational Podcast-Ready Tone)

Picture this. You're a pineapple grower in Ojochal, in the Costa Ballena stretch of the South Pacific, and you're heading into your third planting cycle. Last year you lost a chunk of your crop to root rot in the low corners of the fields — waterlogging, nematodes, fungus, the whole miserable cascade. And the frustrating part is, you know roughly where the water pools, but "roughly" isn't good enough to design a drainage ditch. You need to know exactly where every slope and depression sits across 140 hectares. That's the problem we're getting into today, because the South Pacific is where two of the toughest surveying challenges in Costa Rica — brutal rainfall and complex, steep, forested terrain — come together, and where a drone survey stops being a nice-to-have and becomes a competitiveness and risk-management tool.

So let's set the stage. The South Pacific — from Uvita and Ojochal down the coast to Puerto Jiménez on the Osa Peninsula — is one of the country's most dynamic agricultural and real estate markets at once. Pineapple plantations dominate the landscape, while coastal property and eco-tourism have pushed land values way up. We cover the whole region: the Puntarenas side with Uvita, Ojochal, Dominical, Platanillo, Cortés; the Osa Peninsula with Puerto Jiménez, La Palma, and Carate; the coastal zones around Uvita Point and the Marino Ballena National Park environs; and inland through San Isidro de El General, Buenos Aires, and the broader Pérez Zeledón region.

Let's start with that pineapple problem, because it's the clearest example. Pineapple is high-value but extremely sensitive to drainage — some parts of this region get over 300 inches of rain a year, and waterlogging brings root rot, nematodes, and fungal disease. A lidar-derived elevation model shows exactly where water pools during heavy rain, so growers can design drainage, lay irrigation, and plan field rotation before they ever plant. That prevents crop loss and extends how long a plantation stays productive.

Let me make it concrete with a real case. That 140-hectare producer in Ojochal heading into a third cycle — here's what we delivered. A lidar-derived elevation model at 5-centimeter accuracy showing every slope and depression across all 140 hectares. A contour map with half-meter contour lines revealing the natural water flow. A full-color orthomosaic at 2 centimeters per pixel, showing crop condition, existing drainage, and field boundaries. And an interactive 3D terrain model for planning. Total cost: eighteen hundred dollars, site plus travel. With that elevation data, the grower cut two new drainage ditches along the natural valley lines, stopped the waterlogging in the low fields, and rotated replant cycles to let the soil recover. First-year disease losses dropped 35 percent — the survey paid for itself inside the first harvest.

Now, the second big use here is land and property, because this isn't just farm country. A lot of South Pacific parcels were surveyed decades ago on paper maps with outdated gear, and the terrain is steep and forested, which makes traditional boundary marking unreliable. Aerial orthomosaics and boundary overlays show you the true extent of your land — which you really want to nail down before you buy, subdivide, or develop. And coastal development around Uvita, Ojochal, and Puerto Jiménez often means steep slopes and rivers close by, so a drone survey flags flood-prone areas, landslide risk zones, and natural drainage — the stuff that shapes where you put a building and what the engineering is going to cost.

This is where Osa deserves its own moment, because the Osa Peninsula is one of the most biologically intense places on Earth, and the eco-resort and eco-lodge development down there has to thread a very fine needle. Puerto Jiménez, La Palma, Carate — these are remote, forested, dramatic sites where you're building light-footprint hospitality in some of the densest jungle in the country. That's exactly the terrain where lidar shines. Our DJI Zenmuse L1 penetrates the foliage far better than optical methods, delivering 5-centimeter vertical accuracy even under dense canopy, so you get the real ground elevation under the trees — for drainage, for siting cabins above flood lines, and for documenting that you're building responsibly next to protected land.

And speaking of protected land — a common question is what happens when your property straddles or sits beside a national park. We can survey private land adjacent to parks, like properties near Marino Ballena, and deliver maps suitable for permit applications. What we can't do is survey inside a national park itself without special authorization. So if you're near a boundary, tell us the exact location and we'll confirm feasibility during the quote.

Let's talk crop health for a second, because it's an underrated benefit. Those high-resolution orthomosaics reveal variations you simply can't see from the ground — yellow or brown patches in a pineapple field that flag disease pockets, nutrient deficiency, or a drainage problem. Catch that early and you can intervene in a targeted way instead of losing whole blocks. For pineapple farms specifically we prioritize tight contour accuracy, highest-resolution orthomosaics with RGB and multispectral options, and delivery in GIS formats — GeoTIFF, shapefiles — so it drops straight into farm management software like ArcGIS or QGIS.

A quick note on regulation, because permitting is real here. Land development in Puntarenas needs permits from MINAE — the environmental ministry — and INVU, the housing institute. A drone survey speeds that up by handing you the environmental and topographic baseline those applications require, and we deliver certified outputs suitable for official submission.

Now weather and timing, because the South Pacific is rainy and you have to respect it. May through November is the rainy season — cloud cover and mud can delay flights — so we recommend scheduling big projects in the dry season, December through April, for the cleanest results, though we can work year-round with advance notice. And plan for the trip: San José to the Southern Zone is a five-to-six-hour haul, so travel fees typically run three hundred to five hundred dollars depending on exactly where you are.

On cost, the structure's simple and the same as the rest of the country: surveys start at a thousand dollars for sites up to five hectares, then eighty dollars per hectare beyond that, with that travel fee included in your quote. For projects over a hundred hectares or multi-site campaigns, we do custom pricing.

So whether you're a pineapple producer trying to stop losing crops to standing water, a buyer verifying the real boundaries of a steep forested lot, or a developer siting an eco-lodge on the Osa Peninsula, this is the terrain data that protects your investment in a region where the land does not forgive guesswork. Head to dronesurveycr.com/quote, tell us your size, location, and use case, and we'll respond within 24 hours — or drop us a pin on WhatsApp and we'll talk through the best approach.
