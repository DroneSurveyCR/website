## SOURCE MATERIAL (Conversational Podcast-Ready Tone)

Here's the thing about the Central Valley. More than half the entire population of Costa Rica lives in this one elevated plateau — San José, Alajuela, Cartago, and Heredia, the four provinces ringing the Valle Central. Three million-plus people in a basin sitting between 900 and 1,400 meters elevation, with volcanic mountains on one side and the Fila de Bustamante on the other. So when you've got that many people in one place, what does that mean? It means constant demand for new housing, commercial space, roads, and infrastructure. And every single one of those projects starts with the same question: what does the land actually look like? That's where we come in. Today we're getting into how drone surveys are quietly powering the construction boom across the heart of Costa Rica.

So let's set the scene province by province, because each one has its own flavor. Start with Alajuela. Alajuela has become a magnet for free trade zone development — the zonas francas. Logistics centers, manufacturing plants, tech campuses going up along the General Cañas highway and the Ruta 27 extension. These are often 10- to 50-hectare parcels, and on a site that size, elevation accuracy isn't a nice-to-have. It drives your drainage design, your cut-and-fill calculations, your environmental compliance. Get it wrong and you're paying for it in earthwork later. And it's not just industrial — Grecia, Atenas, San Ramón are booming residentially, pulling in Costa Rican families priced out of San José and foreign retirees chasing the cooler mountain climate. Those subdivisions sit on hilly terrain, and that's exactly where drone-based lidar leaves a ground crew in the dust.

Now Cartago — this is where the agricultural identity is strongest. The upper slopes around Llano Grande, Pacayas, and Tierra Blanca grow the majority of Costa Rica's vegetables and cut flowers. Farms there are using drone surveys for drainage planning, erosion control, and expansion permitting — work that used to mean a topographer spending days walking steep hillsides. And down in the lower elevations, around Cartago city, Tres Ríos, and Paraíso, you've got urban expansion pushing outward, a growing Tecnológico campus, road projects along the Inter-American Highway. All of it needs accurate elevation data.

Then Heredia. Honestly, Heredia's transformation is visible from the air. Former coffee plantations in Barva, San Rafael, and Santo Domingo are being converted into residential and mixed-use developments at a rapid clip. Every one of those conversions needs a pre-construction topographic survey, progress monitoring flights, and usually a final as-built for the municipal sign-off. Plus the UltraPark and Global Park free trade zones near the airport have spurred road and utility work running north into the province.

Let's talk about how construction teams actually use this data, because it's not one survey and done — it spans the whole project. Pre-construction: architects and civil engineers need a centimeter-accurate digital terrain model to design foundations, drainage, retaining walls, and access roads. That DTM feeds straight into AutoCAD Civil 3D or Revit, and on hilly Central Valley sites — which is most of them — it's dramatically faster than a ground crew with a total station. You get contour maps at half-meter or one-meter intervals, slope analysis, and cut-and-fill volume estimates. And for a developer deciding whether to even buy a property, that cut-and-fill number answers the make-or-break question: how much earthwork is this actually going to require?

During construction, periodic flights let project managers track earthwork against the plan. This one's big — on large residential developments in Alajuela and Heredia, earthwork contractors get paid by volume. A monthly drone survey gives you an independent measurement of how much material has actually moved. No more relying on the contractor's self-reported numbers. And if a dispute comes up, or a regulatory inspection's looming, you've got timestamped, georeferenced evidence of exactly what the site looked like on any given date.

And then post-construction — the as-built. Municipal governments across the valley require as-built documentation for final occupancy permits. A drone survey at completion hands you that topographic data plus ortho imagery showing the finished project in context. One flight, permit-ready.

Now there's one thing that makes the Central Valley genuinely different from the rest of the country: airspace. Juan Santamaría International in Alajuela and Tobías Bolaños in Pavas create controlled airspace zones that require coordination with DGAC — the Dirección General de Aviación Civil — before we fly. That doesn't mean we can't operate there. It means we plan ahead, submit flight authorization requests, and coordinate with air traffic control for approved windows. We handle all that paperwork as standard service. The only thing you'd notice is projects right next to the airports may need a few extra days of lead time.

Let's get to cost, because this is home territory for us, so the pricing's straightforward. Base price is a thousand dollars, covering sites up to five hectares — that includes the flight, the GPS base station, processing, and the full deliverable set. Beyond that it's eighty dollars per hectare. And here's the kicker for the Central Valley: most locations within about 90 minutes of San José carry no travel surcharge at all. More remote spots like Turrialba or San Ramón might have a small fee, but we quote it upfront. For the big stuff — a hundred hectares and up — we do custom pricing.

So whether you're standing on a 30-hectare zona franca parcel in Alajuela, converting old coffee land in Heredia, or trying to figure out the earthwork on a hillside subdivision in Grecia, this is the data that gets your project designed, monitored, and permitted.
