## SOURCE MATERIAL (Conversational Podcast-Ready Tone)

So here's a question we get all the time from clients in Costa Rica: *Do I actually need a permit to fly a drone for my survey project?* And the answer is yes — absolutely, one hundred percent yes. Commercial drone operations in Costa Rica are regulated by the DGAC, which stands for the Dirección General de Aviación Civil. They're the country's civil aviation authority, and they have a very clear framework for how drones can be used commercially.

Now, the important distinction here is between flying a drone as a hobby and flying one for business. If there's any form of payment or business benefit involved — you're doing a survey for a developer, mapping a construction site, photographing a property listing — that's a commercial operation, and you need full DGAC authorization. No exceptions. And this applies to everyone, regardless of nationality. There's actually a really common misconception among foreign investors who bring a consumer drone from overseas thinking they can use it for property inspections under tourist status. That's not how it works. Costa Rica's aviation law applies to everyone on Costa Rican soil.

So what does it take to be a fully authorized commercial drone operator? There are four main pillars. First, the pilot needs a valid DGAC UAS operator certification — that involves demonstrating knowledge of aviation regulations, airspace management, meteorology, all of it. Second, every drone used commercially has to be registered with the DGAC — each one gets a registration number that has to be visible on the airframe. Third, you need liability insurance covering property damage and personal injury. And fourth, you have to file a flight plan before every single commercial operation.

Now let's talk about the zones where things get extra complicated. The biggest one people run into is the eight-kilometer exclusion zone around airports. Costa Rica has two international airports — Juan Santamaría near San José and Daniel Oduber in Liberia — plus a bunch of smaller domestic aerodromes scattered across the country. If your property is within eight clicks of any of them, you can still get a drone survey done, but the coordination with air traffic control is more involved and altitude is typically capped at fifty meters without additional approval.

Then there's the national parks and protected areas situation. Costa Rica has an incredible network of protected land managed by SINAC, and if you're surveying near or within one of those areas, you need a dual permit — one from DGAC and a separate environmental permit from SINAC. This comes up a lot, especially with environmental impact assessments and surveys of properties that border protected forests. It adds time, but it's completely manageable when you have an operator with established relationships at the regional SINAC offices.

Urban areas have their own set of rules too. Flying over densely populated zones — residential neighborhoods, commercial districts — requires specific authorization beyond the standard commercial permit. The concern is safety if something goes wrong mid-flight. Most survey work in urban areas can be planned to avoid overflight of uninvolved people, but you need that extra layer of approval.

On altitude: the standard ceiling for drones in uncontrolled airspace is four hundred feet — that's about 120 meters above ground level. For most photogrammetry and LiDAR work, that's plenty. If you need to go higher for a large-area LiDAR mission, you can get site-specific authorization. And all commercial flights have to maintain visual line of sight — the pilot has to be able to see the drone at all times.

Here's where the practical timeline matters for people planning projects. From the moment you confirm a survey with us, we handle the entire permit process. Site assessment happens the same day. Flight plan preparation takes a day or two. DGAC submission another couple of days. Then DGAC processing is usually five to fifteen business days for standard operations. If you're near an airport or a protected area, that dual-permit process can add one to three additional weeks. So for most standard projects, you're looking at ten to eighteen business days from confirmation to flight day. For more complex locations, budget three to five weeks.

Now, why does any of this matter if you're a developer or landowner? Because if you hire an unlicensed operator to save a few bucks, you're taking on serious risk. Survey data collected without proper DGAC authorization might not be accepted for permitting or EIA filings. If something goes wrong — a drone damages property or injures someone — the liability exposure falls on everyone involved, potentially including the property owner. And DGAC can confiscate equipment and issue fines. The cost difference between a licensed operator and an informal one is usually trivial compared to the project budget, but the risk difference is enormous.

As for what's new in 2026 — DGAC has been tightening things up. Remote ID requirements are rolling out for commercial drones in certain airspace classes. Urban overflight documentation requirements got stricter, especially around the San José metro area. Insurance minimums are under review with expected increases. And Costa Rica has been working on bilateral airspace agreements with neighboring countries for cross-border projects. We track all of these changes continuously so clients never have to.

The bottom line: regulations exist to keep everyone safe, and they're not going away — if anything, they're getting more comprehensive. But when you work with a licensed operator, you never deal with any of it. All of the DGAC paperwork, the SINAC permits, the flight plans, the insurance — it's all handled behind the scenes. Your job is just to tell us where your property is and what you need mapped.
