You've heard of LiDAR drone surveys — but what actually happens from the moment we show up on your property to the moment you get your files?

[SECTION: Mission planning]

Before we ever get on a plane to your project, we do the mission planning virtually. We use satellite imagery and elevation data to map out the exact flight path — altitude, speed, overlap between passes — everything optimized for your terrain and the accuracy you need.

For a 10-hectare site, this takes a couple of hours in the office. For a 200-hectare agricultural project, it might be a full day of planning. Getting this right is what gives you centimeter-level results instead of meter-level guesses.

[SECTION: Equipment setup]

On the day of the survey, our first job on site is the GPS base station. We set up an RTK base station at a known control point — this is what anchors every single laser pulse in your survey to real-world coordinates. Without it, you'd have a great-looking point cloud that nobody could actually use.

The drone we fly is the DJI Matrice with a Zenmuse L1 LiDAR payload. The L1 fires 240,000 laser pulses per second and captures up to 3 returns per pulse — meaning it can hit a leaf, bounce through to a branch, and still hit the ground. That's how it maps through forest canopy.

[SECTION: The flight]

The flight itself looks almost boring from the ground. The drone follows its pre-programmed path, back and forth, maintaining a precise altitude above the terrain. Depending on the size of the project, a flight might take 20 minutes or a full day with battery changes.

What's happening in the air is anything but boring. Every second, the LiDAR is firing hundreds of thousands of laser pulses, the IMU is tracking every tiny movement of the drone, and the GPS is logging position data at centimeter accuracy.

[SECTION: Data processing]

Back at our office, this is where the real work happens. We run the raw data through processing software to produce the point cloud — which is essentially millions of dots in 3D space, each one representing a laser return from your terrain.

From there we classify the points. Ground points, vegetation, structures. We generate a bare-earth digital elevation model — the terrain surface with everything else removed. Then we produce your contour lines, your DXF file for CAD, your GeoTIFF for GIS.

This typically takes 3 to 7 business days depending on project size.

[SECTION: Deliverables]

A LAS or LAZ point cloud — the raw 3D data, usable in any LiDAR software.

A GeoTIFF orthomosaic — a georeferenced aerial image.

Everything delivered digitally, usually through a shared drive link.

[SECTION: Accuracy]

How accurate is it? Our LiDAR surveys achieve horizontal accuracy of 3 to 5 centimeters and vertical accuracy of 5 to 10 centimeters. For comparison, a traditional ground survey with a total station gets you similar accuracy — but for a fraction of the time and cost per hectare at scale.

[SECTION: Who needs a permit?]

First — who needs a commercial drone permit?

If you're flying a drone and getting paid for it — or if you're using drone footage for any business purpose — you need a commercial operator license from DGAC. Recreational flying under 250 grams has different, lighter rules. But the moment you're doing surveys, real estate photography, or mapping for a client, you're in commercial territory.

[SECTION: DGAC registration]

To operate commercially, you need to register with DGAC — the Dirección General de Aviación Civil. This involves registering your drone, providing proof of pilot training, submitting an operations manual, and carrying aviation liability insurance.

The process takes several weeks and involves submitting documentation through DGAC's online portal. They issue you an operator number which must be marked on your aircraft.

[SECTION: Restricted zones]

Not everywhere in Costa Rica is open to drone flights. There are restricted zones you need to know about.

Within 9 kilometers of any commercial airport — Juan Santamaría, Daniel Oduber, Tobías Bolaños — drone flights require prior coordination with air traffic control. That covers a big chunk of the Central Valley.

National parks and protected areas have their own restrictions — most require a special permit from SINAC in addition to DGAC authorization.

Border zones within 50 kilometers of Panama or Nicaragua have additional military airspace considerations.

And anywhere with a density of people — stadiums, public events, urban areas — requires additional operational justification.

[SECTION: Insurance]

Insurance is not optional. DGAC requires aviation liability insurance for all commercial drone operations in Costa Rica. The minimum coverage amount depends on your drone's takeoff weight. For a professional LiDAR system like the DJI Matrice with Zenmuse L1, you're looking at a heavier aircraft that requires higher coverage limits.

[SECTION: Penalties]

What happens if you fly commercially without the right permits? DGAC can fine you, ground your operation, and in serious cases, confiscate equipment. Beyond the regulatory risk, you also have no insurance coverage — so if something goes wrong on a job site, you're personally liable.
